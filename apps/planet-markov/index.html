With few training pictures, most of what the model sees appears in only one of them, so it copies that picture. Learning from every flip and turn gives it up to 8 versions of each, so fewer patterns are unique to one picture. The closest-match line under the imitation shows how much it still copies.

The seed is a random training picture shrunk to 9 pixels. Upscaler stages grow it to 96 px, so the big shapes get decided while the image is tiny. The painter instead builds the 96 px picture row by row from Neighbors, which suits pictures that are all texture, like mazes and star fields.

What the model sees

Planets are drawn from above the pole, so for them distance from the center is latitude. Upscaler stages get it too.

Upscaling

The 96 px planet sets the big shapes. Each stage then doubles it with a second counting model: every new pixel looks at the low-res pixel it came from, which corner of it it is, and the pixels to its left and above. Stages learn from 3 pictures at 768 px. Star fields have none: shrinking them erases the spikes, so they stay at 96 px.

    This model paints one pixel at a time, left to right, top to bottom. For each pixel it looks up what colors came next after the same surroundings in the training pictures, and rolls a die weighted by those counts. That is the whole model.

    That makes it generative AI in its simplest form: everything it paints comes from statistics learned from example pictures. The Galaxy Photo Generator is the opposite, a procedural algorithm with hand-written rules and no training data.
