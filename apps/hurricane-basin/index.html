Climate

Hurricanes run on evaporation from water warmer than about 26.5 °C. The Sea temperature layer marks that line in white. Warmer water gives storms more fuel; cooler water starves them.

The trade winds blow storms west in the tropics; the westerlies farther north sweep them back east. Turn them off to watch storms drift on their own: still north-west, because the spin of the Earth changes with latitude.

How it works

The atmosphere here is one layer of air on a spinning Earth, solved on a 256 × 128 grid of 35 km cells. There is no hurricane code in it. Every storm comes out of five rules:

  • Pressure pushes air from high to low, and the spin of the Earth turns moving air to the right, so lows spin counter-clockwise.
  • Warm ocean evaporates water, faster where the wind is stronger.
  • Where moist air piles up, it rains. Rain releases heat, the heated air rises out of the layer, and pressure drops. Lower pressure pulls in more moist air. That loop is what grows a hurricane.
  • Lifted air sinks again nearby, warming and drying a ring around each storm. That keeps storms compact and leaves the clear moat you see around them.
  • Land and friction drain energy: little evaporation over land, and rough ground slows the wind.

Things to try:

  • Drop two hurricanes a few hundred km apart and watch them orbit each other, then merge: the Fujiwhara effect.
  • Watch a storm curve: west with the trade winds, north around the Bermuda High, then east into the westerlies.
  • Steer one into Florida or Mexico and watch it fall apart over land.
  • Cool the ocean by 3 °C and see how few disturbances become storms.

Categories use the Saffir-Simpson scale on the strongest wind near the centre. Cells are 35 km wide, so the calm eye you see is several times wider than a real one, and intensities are rougher than the tracks.
