Every cell starts as the same simple alga: it photosynthesises and takes up nutrients. A genome is a list of cell types, each with its own traits and a rule for dividing: which type the child becomes, at what angle, and whether it stays attached. Attached cells form one body with one genome, can share energy, and face the way their parent faced, so swimmers in a body push together. Every cell carries a signal between −1 and +1, worked out from what it senses, how that is changing, an inner rhythm and the signals of the cells it touches. The signal sets how hard a swimming cell pushes and which way it turns, so a body's cells can act like a small nervous system. Cells that break free carry slightly mutated genes, including how fast they mutate, and types can be copied and then drift apart. Genes cost energy to keep, so nothing is free. Light is strongest at the top and nutrients sink, rising again only at two upwellings. Dead cells and scraps return to the water, so the total amount of matter never changes. There is no score: whatever survives and divides is what you see.
