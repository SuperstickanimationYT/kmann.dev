Sail the Stars

Fly anywhere, from the surface of a moon to the far side of the Milky Way. Tap or click anything in view, or type a name under Select, then press Warp. For a challenge, switch the mode to Mission.

A units bug in the navigation software sent humanity's first crewed warp ship 63,000 times too far, out past the Milky Way. Find the Sun and click it. Warp jumps are blind and burn fuel you cannot refill. The mission clock never stops; jumps and exposures add their length and every wrong claim costs an hour.

What is real and what is made up

Planet and moon positions and spins are real for today's date. From 2050 to 3000 the planets stay within about a quarter of a degree of NASA's, going by the dates we checked, but the smaller moons drift. After 3000, in our checks at 4500 and 9500, Mercury to Saturn stay within half a degree, the Moon within a degree, Uranus within about 6 and Neptune within about 5; eclipses that late land in the wrong place. Earth, Moon and Mars surfaces come from real NASA maps (about 39, 11 and 21 km per pixel); finer detail is made up by the game, including the exact outlines of light and dark patches, which the maps only show blurred, and the hills and small craters you see after landing. Every rock and blade of grass is made up too: grass grows where the map of Earth, or of terraformed Venus, is green, and Titan's pebbles are rounded like those Huygens photographed. The courses of Earth's large rivers are real, from Natural Earth's maps; their widths, depths, small bends and valleys are made up. Rivers on terraformed Venus and on other stars' planets follow the lie of the land from high ground to the sea, and lava rivers on the hottest planets run down to the lowest ground; all of those are made up. Fly down to land on any of the three. Landing turns on Follow and Rotate with, so you stay on the spot you reached; either can be turned off. Earth's clouds are real ones: a NASA satellite (VIIRS on Suomi NPP, through NASA GIBS) photographed them on 21 March 2019. Beyond about 80°N and 76°S, where it saw no daylight, they come from MODIS on NASA's Aqua satellite the same day, pieced together from passes hours apart, which leaves some straight edges. Their shapes stay frozen at that day, but the game carries them all eastward around the globe once every 40 days, so they drift off the coasts and mountains they formed over. Shapes finer than about 13 km and their heights up close are made up. Earth's city lights are real too, from NASA's Black Marble map of 2016; single lamps, shapes finer than about 5 km, their colours, their glow on clouds and their growth since 1882 are made up. Venus shows its real, nearly featureless cloud tops, which hide its surface; their faint streaks are made up. Titan's orange haze also hides its surface; its faint haze patterns are made up. You can fly down through both and land. Venus's hills come from Magellan radar, but its rock colour is a guess, since no camera has mapped it. Titan's hills come from Cassini radar, which measured only about 9% of the surface; the rest is filled in. Its ground brightness comes from Cassini's infrared camera. The glow of air around Earth, Mars, Venus and Titan is worked out from how sunlight scatters in each one's air, with strengths tuned by eye. Saturn's rings use their real radii, gaps and thicknesses, and cast and catch real shadows; their fine ringlets are made up. Solar and lunar eclipses happen on their real dates and paths: the Moon matches NASA's ephemeris to within about 20 arcseconds (roughly 40 km on the ground). During totality the corona appears; its streamers are made up. The four giant planets' swirls are painted by the game, flowing along made-up winds. They keep drifting and changing under a thin layer of high cloud, much faster than the real ones so you can see it; pausing time stops them. Their band positions and colours are tuned by eye to real photos, and the Great Red Spot, Saturn's hexagon and other features sit at about their real latitudes but made-up longitudes. Other planets and moons are placeholders until their maps are added, though every moon follows its real orbit. Dwarf planets follow JPL's orbits (within 0.7° from 1700 to 2400 for Ceres, 0.2° for the rest); their faraway dates drift more. Ceres, Pluto and Charon use real maps from Dawn and New Horizons, tinted by eye. Nobody has seen the far south of Pluto and Charon, so that part is made up, and so are the heights over most of their far sides; Ceres has only made-up hills. Pluto's haze is drawn brighter than it is. Every other dwarf planet and its moons have made-up surfaces in roughly their measured colours and sizes, as nobody has seen them up close. Haumea's stretched shape comes from its occultation of a star in 2017, but which way its long end points is a guess. Orcus, Quaoar, Gonggong and Sedna are likely dwarf planets, not officially agreed ones. Haumea's moons follow their real orbits but may be at the wrong place along them; the spin poles of Makemake, Gonggong and Sedna are guesses, and some small moons and faint rings are left out. Type Voyager 1, Curiosity or Apollo 11 under Select to visit a spacecraft, lander or rover. Thirty-four spacecraft follow paths from NASA's JPL Horizons service for as long as JPL lists them, squeezed to about 0.3 MB; they are typically within 0.1% of JPL's distance from whatever they circle, and a few percent off in the days around a flyby or arrival. Where a low orbit is drawn round Earth, the Moon or Mars, its size and tilt are real but the place along it is approximate. After JPL's data ends, craft still orbiting or drifting simply carry on. Thirty-eight landers and rovers stand at their published coordinates. Rovers move between a few dated stops in a straight line; the real routes wind about. The ground around them is the game's own, so it won't match their photos. Every model is simplified and built by hand from published sizes, and which way each craft points is worked out from its job (dish to Earth, panels to the Sun). Spacecraft on the dark side get a faint light so you can see them. Landers on comets and asteroids are left out because those worlds aren't in the game. Another 193 stars have their known planets, including nearly all hosts brighter than magnitude 6.6: type TRAPPIST-1, Pollux or 47 Ursae Majoris under Select. Planets NASA marks as disputed are left out. Star positions, sizes and temperatures, and planet sizes, masses, years and orbit sizes come from the NASA Exoplanet Archive; “about” and “at least” mark numbers nobody has measured directly. Planets that pass in front of their star do so at the measured times. Made up: which way each orbit is turned on the sky, missing tilts and positions, spins (close planets are assumed to keep one side to their star), and every surface, sea, cloud and crater, though lava, ice and seas follow each planet's temperature. No one has seen an exoplanet's surface. Young giants photographed directly, such as those of HR 8799 and Beta Pictoris, glow with the colour of their measured heat. You can land on the rocky ones: their hills, coasts, craters and lava cracks are made up too, and so is the air over the mild ones. Their clouds, and those of terraformed Venus, are ten maps from a simplified weather simulation of an Earth-like planet, reused from world to world. Every other star except the young ones in nebulae can have procedurally generated planets; the panel says so when you click one, and the same star always has the same planets. They are not real, only plausible and consistent with physics. How many there are, their sizes and their spacing follow survey results: close-in small planets a model fitted to Kepler's finds (He and Ford 2026), those of red dwarfs Dressing and Charbonneau 2015, giants Fulton 2021 and Rosenthal 2022, cold small planets a count from microlensing (Zang 2025). In a test of 20,000 F, G and K stars, 87% had a close-in small planet, 14 giants turned up per 100 stars between 2 and 8 AU, and 0.4% had a hot Jupiter. Planets of 1 to 4 Earth radii around red dwarfs came out about a fifth too rare, and giants too weakly tied to small inner planets. Planets too close together to last are thrown out, and so are those within 0.4 AU of swollen giant stars. Stars heavier than 3 Suns get none. Companion stars limit planets only where the star catalogue lists them, and the orbits ignore planets' pull on each other. Simulated stars are named SIM and a number; unnamed real ones by their place in Earth's sky. Black holes: type Sagittarius A*, OGLE-2011-BLG-0462, Nearest black hole or Nearest black hole with planets under Select. All but the first two are simulated: about 120 million in the Milky Way (Olejak 2020), each placed at random where old stars are, with a mass worked out from the star it was (Spera 2015). Their planets are procedurally generated too. They form like those of a 3-Sun star, any that came nearer than the star's largest radius (Romagnolo 2023, its largest estimate) are removed, and the rest move outward as the star loses mass. Kicks from the collapse, companions and gas falling in are left out, so these black holes are dark and only bend light. No light but the sky's reaches their planets, drawn a little brighter than it would be; their temperatures are their own inner heat. Sagittarius A* has its measured mass and place. The gas around it is drawn from models, since no telescope sees it in visible light: hot, thick and see-through, brighter on the side turning toward you. Its clumps, exact shape and tilt are made up, and its brightness is carried over from infrared; the orange of the 2022 picture stands for radio waves. Light paths follow general relativity for a black hole that does not spin, seen from a ship standing still; planets behind one are bent as if they were very far away, and the colour shift of the sky close in is left out. The ship stops at 1.2 times the horizon's radius. With Speculative on, the Moon and Mars gain lights as the date runs forward to the year 3000; every place, date and number there is a guess. The Moon only gets a few dots, about 25 stations with 25,000 people at most, because its ice is thought too scarce for cities even with careful recycling. A first base stands near its south pole in the 2030s, as NASA and China plan. Mars has far more ice: people land in the 2040s, about 1,500 live there in 2100, 100,000 in 2200 and 100 million in 3000, mostly near its buried ice and low ground. The lights are drawn brighter than such small places would look. Venus is terraformed from 2400 to 9500, going by published ideas, with dates we made up. A sunshade of small mirrors, 25,000 km wide, grows between Venus and the Sun until 2560. In its shade the planet cools; by 2740 the clouds are gone, from 2760 the carbon dioxide rains out as seas, and by 3000 those have frozen. Shipped hydrogen then turns the dry ice into water and carbon, which takes about 2,300 years. A Venus day lasts 117 Earth days, so from 5300 a second mirror circles Venus once a day and stands in for the Sun. The shade stays to block the real one. The ice melts, plants grow from 5800, people move in from 5900, and the air can be breathed by 7500. Coasts follow Magellan's radar heights with the sea filled to the level its water would reach; clouds, plants and cities are made up. LHS 1140 b and TOI-700 d and e show city lights on their night sides. The lamps take their star's colour, on the idea that eyes evolved under it would see that colour as white. No sign of life has been found on any planet but Earth; these three were chosen because they are rocky, warm enough for liquid water in the game, heavy enough that we guess they have a magnetic field, and circle quieter stars than Proxima or TRAPPIST-1. Procedurally generated planets that pass the same tests for size and warmth can have cities too. The game guesses generously: wherever 4.5 billion steady years have passed, as they have for Earth, it puts a civilization that still lasts. Each star gets an age picked evenly from 0 to 10 billion years, or to the end of its life if sooner. The wait starts when its flares calm down (after 0.8 to 7 billion years for red dwarfs, roughly following West and others 2008), and the planet must have been warm enough all that time while its star brightened. About 1 star in 20 near the Sun then has one. Nobody knows the true share: if civilizations lasted only 100 years, Westby and Conselice (2020) expect about 36 in the whole Galaxy. Type “Nearest civilization” under Select to visit the closest. Four wormhole mouths appear too, in two pairs. They are made up: general relativity allows the shape, but nobody has seen one or knows how to make one, so they stay hidden unless Speculative is on. The first pair, 100 m in radius, joins the Sun's planets to Proxima Centauri from 6043, after the far mouth has been carried there for 43 years at a tenth of light speed. The second, 300 m in radius, joins LHS 1140 to TOI-700 at every date and was built by the made-up civilization of LHS 1140 b. Each mouth keeps 60° ahead of a planet on its orbit, where small things stay put: Earth, Proxima b, LHS 1140 b and TOI-700 d. Type Wormhole under Select to find them, then fly straight in. Their shape is the simplest known one (Ellis 1973), and light through and around them follows general relativity worked out from it, as in James, von Tunzelmann, Franklin and Thorne 2015. It has no gravity, so nothing pulls the ship, stretches it or slows its clock, and you need no speed to cross. Holding one open takes matter with negative energy, about 2 × 10⁴⁴ joules of it per metre of radius: a tenth of the Sun's whole mass turned to energy for the first pair. No such matter is known, and quantum rules worked out by Ford and Roman suggest that a throat that wide would need it squeezed into an absurdly thin layer. Two wormhole mouths could also make a time machine; the game ignores that, and keeps one date everywhere. Planets on the far side show as points of light, the mouths cast no light on nearby worlds, and long-exposure photos leave them out.

Warp drive

Jumps go along the ship's nose, the middle of the big front window, and land up to 2% of the distance off target. As the bubble forms, the sky squeezes ahead of you and turns blue until the microwave background glows white-hot; blast shutters close against it and open again as you drop out. The drive is stuck at the bugged speed, about 1,500 ly/s, and burns fuel for every second of warp. You have 4:30 of warp, 1.5× the trip home, so plan before you jump. Thrusters are free but top out at 10 ly/s.

Camera

Camera framed like your view: a 36 mm lens at the normal zoom, a longer lens of the same opening when you zoom in. It stacks 10 s frames; each takes 1 s of real time and adds 10 s to the clock. Your ship holds still until the exposure ends or you stop it.

True size

Measure the lit part of your screen from side to side, then from your eyes to the screen, and sit with your eyes in front of the middle of the view. The view then covers — top to bottom, the angle a window that size would. Zooming changes it; 0 or the field of view button brings it back. Keep the browser at 100% zoom, since the screen's size is worked out from the pixels it reports.

Stars are procedural, except about 7,800 naked-eye stars within 3,000 light-years from Hipparcos. Nebulae: the 50 most ionizing star-forming complexes in Russeil (2003) plus Orion, Eagle, Lagoon, Rosette, North America and California, with rough distances and brightness. Spiral arms follow Reid et al. (2019). Everything you see is where it was when its light left, and the galaxy turns while that light travels. The warp view comes from light rays traced through an Alcubierre bubble 100 m across with a 10 m wall. The cutscene music is Koto garden from Song Studio, sped up from 72 to 200 bpm.
