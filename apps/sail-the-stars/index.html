Sail the Stars

Fly anywhere, from the surface of a moon to the far side of the Milky Way. Click anything in view, or type a name under Select, then press Warp. For a challenge, switch the mode to Mission.

A units bug in the navigation software sent humanity's first crewed warp ship 63,000 times too far, out past the Milky Way. Find the Sun and click it. Warp jumps are blind and burn fuel you cannot refill. The mission clock never stops; jumps and exposures add their length and every wrong claim costs an hour.

Planet and moon positions and spins are real for today's date. From 2050 to 3000 the planets stay within about a quarter of a degree of NASA's, going by the dates we checked, but the smaller moons drift. After 3000, in our checks at 4500 and 9500, Mercury to Saturn stay within half a degree, the Moon within a degree, Uranus within about 6 and Neptune within about 5; eclipses that late land in the wrong place. Earth, Moon and Mars surfaces come from real NASA maps (about 39, 11 and 21 km per pixel); finer detail is made up by the game, including the hills and small craters you see after landing. Fly down to land on any of the three. Landing turns on Follow and Rotate with, so you stay on the spot you reached; either can be turned off. Earth's clouds are real ones: a NASA satellite (VIIRS on Suomi NPP, through NASA GIBS) photographed them on 21 March 2019. Their shapes stay frozen at that day, but the game carries them all eastward around the globe once every 40 days, so they drift off the coasts and mountains they formed over. Shapes finer than about 13 km, their heights up close and the clouds over the poles are made up. Earth's city lights are real too, from NASA's Black Marble map of 2016; single lamps, shapes finer than about 5 km, their colours, their glow on clouds and their growth since 1882 are made up. Venus shows its real, nearly featureless cloud tops, which hide its surface; their faint streaks are made up. Titan's orange haze also hides its surface; its faint haze patterns are made up. You can fly down through both and land. Venus's hills come from Magellan radar, but its rock colour is a guess, since no camera has mapped it. Titan's hills come from Cassini radar, which measured only about 9% of the surface; the rest is filled in. Its ground brightness comes from Cassini's infrared camera. The glow of air around Earth, Mars, Venus and Titan is worked out from how sunlight scatters in each one's air, with strengths tuned by eye. Saturn's rings use their real radii, gaps and thicknesses, and cast and catch real shadows; their fine ringlets are made up. Solar and lunar eclipses happen on their real dates and paths: the Moon matches NASA's ephemeris to within about 20 arcseconds (roughly 40 km on the ground). During totality the corona appears; its streamers are made up. The four giant planets' swirls are painted by the game, flowing along made-up winds. They keep drifting and changing under a thin layer of high cloud, much faster than the real ones so you can see it; pausing time stops them. Their band positions and colours are tuned by eye to real photos, and the Great Red Spot, Saturn's hexagon and other features sit at about their real latitudes but made-up longitudes. Other planets and moons are placeholders until their maps are added, though every moon follows its real orbit. Fifteen other stars have their known planets: type TRAPPIST-1, Proxima Centauri or 55 Cancri under Select. Star positions, sizes and temperatures, and planet sizes, masses, years and orbit sizes come from the NASA Exoplanet Archive; “about” and “at least” mark numbers nobody has measured directly. Planets that pass in front of their star do so at the measured times. Made up: which way each orbit is turned on the sky, missing tilts and positions, spins (close planets are assumed to keep one side to their star), and every surface, sea, cloud and crater, though lava, ice and seas follow each planet's temperature. No one has seen an exoplanet's surface. The young giants of HR 8799 and Beta Pictoris glow with the colour of their measured heat. You can land on the rocky ones: their hills, coasts, craters and lava cracks are made up too, and so is the air over the mild ones. Their clouds, and those of terraformed Venus, are ten maps from a simplified weather simulation of an Earth-like planet, reused from world to world. With Speculative on, the Moon and Mars gain lights as the date runs forward to the year 3000; every place, date and number there is a guess. The Moon only gets a few dots, about 25 stations with 25,000 people at most, because its ice is thought too scarce for cities even with careful recycling. A first base stands near its south pole in the 2030s, as NASA and China plan. Mars has far more ice: people land in the 2040s, about 1,500 live there in 2100, 100,000 in 2200 and 100 million in 3000, mostly near its buried ice and low ground. The lights are drawn brighter than such small places would look. Venus is terraformed from 2400 to 9500, going by published ideas, with dates we made up. A sunshade of small mirrors, 25,000 km wide, grows between Venus and the Sun until 2560. In its shade the planet cools; by 2740 the clouds are gone, from 2760 the carbon dioxide rains out as seas, and by 3000 those have frozen. Shipped hydrogen then turns the dry ice into water and carbon, which takes about 2,300 years. A Venus day lasts 117 Earth days, so from 5300 a second mirror circles Venus once a day and stands in for the Sun. The shade stays to block the real one. The ice melts, plants grow from 5800, people move in from 5900, and the air can be breathed by 7500. Coasts follow Magellan's radar heights with the sea filled to the level its water would reach; clouds, plants and cities are made up. LHS 1140 b and TOI-700 d and e show city lights on their night sides. The lamps take their star's colour, on the idea that eyes evolved under it would see that colour as white. No sign of life has been found on any planet but Earth; these three were chosen because they are rocky, warm enough for liquid water in the game, heavy enough that we guess they have a magnetic field, and circle quieter stars than Proxima or TRAPPIST-1.

Warp drive

Jumps go along the ship's nose, the middle of the big front window, and land up to 2% of the distance off target. As the bubble forms, the sky squeezes ahead of you and turns blue until the microwave background glows white-hot; blast shutters close against it and open again as you drop out. The drive is stuck at the bugged speed, about 1,500 ly/s, and burns fuel for every second of warp. You have 4:30 of warp, 1.5× the trip home, so plan before you jump. Thrusters are free but top out at 10 ly/s.

Camera

Camera framed like your view: a 36 mm lens at the normal zoom, a longer lens of the same opening when you zoom in. It stacks 10 s frames; each takes 1 s of real time and adds 10 s to the clock. Your ship holds still until the exposure ends or you stop it.

Stars are procedural, except about 7,800 naked-eye stars within 3,000 light-years from Hipparcos. Nebulae: the 50 most ionizing star-forming complexes in Russeil (2003) plus Orion, Eagle, Lagoon, Rosette, North America and California, with rough distances and brightness. Spiral arms follow Reid et al. (2019). Everything you see is where it was when its light left, and the galaxy turns while that light travels. The warp view comes from light rays traced through an Alcubierre bubble 100 m across with a 10 m wall. The cutscene music is Koto garden from Song Studio, sped up from 72 to 200 bpm.
