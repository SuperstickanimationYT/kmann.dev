How it works, and where it fails

Stars are found as round bright spots, widest first and then down to the smallest, and each one is painted over out to where its glow fades, using the light around it. If the brightest stars show diffraction spikes, their angles are learned from the photo, and any bright spot with spikes at those angles is removed as a star along with its spikes, however big it looks; a small galaxy has no spikes and stays. The outline is an ellipse fitted to the galaxy's glow. A round disk seen at a tilt looks like an ellipse whose short axis shrinks by the cosine of the tilt, so stretching the short axis back out shows the disk face-on. Real disks have thickness, so a disk seen edge-on still looks about 20% as wide as it is long; the thickness setting accounts for that. Stars are put back unstretched, since they sit in front of the galaxy, not in its disk.

A bulge is roughly a ball, so stretching it turns it into a fake bar; keep it round with the bulge setting. Dust lanes on the near side get stretched into streaks. Above about 70° the far side of the disk is hidden behind the near side, and no stretch brings it back. A photo can't tell which face of the disk points at us, so the spiral may wind the wrong way; flip it with "View from the other side". Tilts for known galaxies come from published measurements and are rounded.
